# Speaker notes: Euro sovereign bonds under pressure (13 to 20 November 2026)

Target: about 70 seconds per content slide at roughly 145 words per minute. Total spoken text is 700 words, which is about 4 minutes 50 seconds.

## Opening (title slide, about 8 seconds)

This week's bond story in one line: energy keeps inflation high, central banks keep tightening, and fiscal risk decides who pays most.

## Slide 1. Inflation (about 70 seconds, 171 words)

It starts with energy. Euro area inflation reached 4.0 percent in October, the highest since September 2023. The final release on 18 November confirmed it, with energy prices up 21 percent on the year and core inflation at 2.6 percent. The chart on the right, from the ECB's new Financial Stability Review, shows why. The dark blue line is the gas price, which spiked after the Middle East war began in March, eased with the April ceasefire, and rose again after it broke down in June. Short-term inflation compensation, the yellow line, moved with gas. Medium-term expectations, the red line, barely moved. So markets still trust the ECB, but only as long as it acts. That is what the table shows. The ECB already hiked in June and September, and markets price about a 91 percent chance of another quarter point on 17 December. In the US, October CPI rose 0.4 percent in the month, above expectations, and a Fed hike on 9 December is priced at 95 to 98 percent.

## Slide 2. Yields (about 70 seconds, 170 words)

Tighter policy lifts yields everywhere. The FSR says advanced-economy yields are close to their highest since the aftermath of the global financial crisis, especially at the long end. On 19 November the ten-year Bund was at 3.41 percent and the ten-year Treasury at 5.04 percent. In the week to 13 November the US two-year rose 37 basis points and the ten-year 29, a classic bear flattening driven by hike expectations. The chart is rebuilt from the ECB's published chart data and splits the rise in two ways. The model split separates expected policy rates from the term premium. The Fisher split separates the real rate from inflation compensation. The two must not be added together. In the euro area the ten-year rose about 47 basis points: roughly 24 from expectations and 22 from term premium, or 25 real and 21 inflation. In the US almost all of the 74 basis points is real rate. So in Europe, inflation risk and the term premium explain about half of the move each.

## Slide 3. Spreads (about 70 seconds, 165 words)

A common shock does not hit everyone equally. On the right is the ECB's chart of ten-year spreads over the Bund, with 2026 enlarged. The yellow line is France. The OAT-Bund spread reached 177 basis points in late October, the highest since November 2011, and was still 153 on 19 November. Italy, in red, has widened too, while the average of the other countries, in blue, stays low by historical standards. The bar chart shows one snapshot from 12 November: France at 148, Italy at 104, Spain at 57. The FSR links this to fiscal fundamentals. Since 2025, spread changes track projected debt-to-GDP ratios, more steeply at the long end. The second concern is who holds the debt. Central banks held 34.9 percent of euro area sovereign debt in 2022 and only 21.5 percent now, while foreign investors rose to 25.1 percent. These buyers are more price sensitive and often leveraged. That is why the ECB warns of a higher risk of abrupt repricing episodes.

## Slide 4. Outlook (about 65 seconds, 153 words)

What should we watch next? The timeline begins with Lagarde's keynote at the European Banking Congress on 20 November. On 25 November the US publishes core PCE and the second estimate of third-quarter GDP. On 1 December Eurostat releases the flash inflation estimate for November, the last big data point before the meetings. Then come the decisions: the Fed on 9 December, with a hike almost fully priced, and the ECB on 17 December, at about 91 percent. Below the timeline are three indicators. First, oil and gas prices, because energy sets short-term inflation and therefore the size of the hikes. Second, the OAT-Bund spread at 153 basis points, because the French budget debate will decide the fiscal premium. Third, demand at long-dated auctions, because weaker demand at the long end is exactly what pushes term premia up. The takeaway: energy keeps policy tight, and fiscal fundamentals decide where the term premium lands.
